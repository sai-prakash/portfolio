I've had most of these titles. UI developer, lead product designer, innovation architect, UX engineer, and now Staff Design Engineer. The job underneath them has been surprisingly consistent: own the seam between how something is designed and how it's built.

Here's how I'd explain the titles to a recruiter, and what to look for when hiring.

## The shared core

Whatever the title, the role exists because something gets lost between a design file and production code. Interaction details, states, accessibility, the feel of a transition. A design engineer is the person who can carry intent across that gap without a handoff, because they work fluently on both sides.

In practice that means three things:

- **They ship production code**, usually front end, to the same standard as the engineering team.
- **They make design decisions**, not just implement them, especially the ones that only show up in a working product.
- **They build the system**, the components, tokens and tooling that make the next hundred screens consistent.

## How the titles tend to differ

| Title | Usually leans toward | Typical home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UX engineer | Prototyping and production UI with a strong research and usability lens | Product teams, Google-style orgs |
| Design engineer | Production components and design systems, high craft | Design systems teams, product startups |
| Design technologist | Exploration, new interaction models, emerging tech | Innovation and R&D groups |
| Front-end engineer (design systems) | Engineering rigour, APIs, performance | Platform teams |

These are tendencies, not definitions. Read the job description, not the title.

## What changed with AI

Two things. First, the seam got wider. AI features need designing at the level of behaviour, not just screens: what the model is allowed to do, how it shows its sources, how a person corrects it. That's design-engineering work.

Second, the design system got a new user. Coding agents now build a lot of UI, and someone has to make the system [readable by agents](/writing/your-design-system-has-a-new-reader) and [measure what they produce](/writing/measure-the-agent-not-the-demo). That's design-engineering work too, and it's where most of mine sits today.

## Hiring for the role

If you're hiring, a portfolio of screens won't tell you much. Ask for:

1. **Something they shipped**, with the code or a working link, and what they decided along the way.
2. **A system they built or changed**, and how they knew it worked.
3. **A disagreement** between design and engineering they resolved, and how.

The best signal is a candidate who talks about both sides of a decision without switching vocabulary.
