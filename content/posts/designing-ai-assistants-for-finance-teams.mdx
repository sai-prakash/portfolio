In finance, the cost of a confident wrong answer is high, and the people doing the work know it. An AI assistant for a finance team has to save time without ever asking them to trust it blindly.

Liquidity Co-Pilot cut one liquidity task from about 218 minutes to about 37. The pattern behind it is simple enough to reuse: the assistant prepares, shows its working, and hands the decision back.

<BeforeAfter label="Time for the liquidity task" before={218} after={37} unit="min" />

## Prepare

Most of the 218 minutes wasn't the decision. It was gathering: finding the right figures, lining them up, reconciling them. That's where an assistant earns its place. Map the task step by step and look for the steps that are search, collation and first drafts. Leave judgement with the person.

## Show

Every number the assistant brings forward should show where it came from. Not a footnote: right next to the value, one click away from the source. Specialists check things; the interface should make checking the fastest path, not an extra one.

When the assistant isn't sure, it should say so plainly, and say what it would need to be sure.

## Hand back

The person approves, edits or rejects. The assistant never acts on its own in a step with financial consequences. This isn't only about risk; it's what makes adoption possible. People adopt tools that make them faster at their job, not tools that try to do their job.

## Earn adoption, don't mandate it

Liquidity Co-Pilot's adoption came from the people who do the task choosing to use it. A few things helped:

- **Start with one task**, end to end, rather than a general assistant.
- **Measure the before.** The 218-minute baseline made the value obvious.
- **Ship to the specialists first**, and change the design based on what they did, not what they said.

## The general rule

If an assistant saves time on gathering and loses it on checking, it saves nothing. Design the checking first.
