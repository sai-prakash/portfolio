## Context

Coding agents write interface code quickly, and they guess. Colours are hard-coded instead of taken from the design system's tokens. Buttons are hand-rolled where a component exists. Labels go missing. None of this is new: people did all of it before agents. Agents just do it faster and in larger volume, and nobody reads that much code.

The question I wanted to answer was simple and had no good answer: when a team changes a prompt, a model or a piece of documentation, did the agent's output actually get better? Without a measure, every improvement is an opinion.

## Role, and the team

I designed and built the checks, and presented them. This started as my own project for an internal design-organisation hackathon in 2026, and I carried it from there. The people who adopted it were designers, product teams and engineers across the design organisation. I do not name teams or colleagues on this page.

## Constraints

- **A regulated bank.** Internal code and screens stay inside the bank, so the tooling has to as well.
- **Apps that were never built on the system.** The interesting targets were the applications teams want to migrate. They do not use the design system's components, so a check that only works on compliant code is useless where it matters most.
- **A moving target.** The thing being graded, an agent's output, changes with every prompt and model. The check has to be stable even though what it grades is not.
- **Another team had built a gate.** A separate engineering team was building a pass or fail validation across the bank. That is a different job from mine, and I had to be clear about the difference.

## What I built

It began as a linter for the hackathon. You point it at an application, in code or running in a browser, and it shows where the design system is being broken. It lived in three places: in the editor, where it flags violations as you type; in the browser, where it scans a running application; and in an agent's workflow, where it checks what the agent has just produced and hands the problems back.

The hackathon presentation mattered more than I expected. I built it as a live, three-dimensional demo with the audio and visuals timed together, and I hardened it for a live room: a recorded video fallback made first, projector presets, a presenter's keyboard controls with an instant skip, and everything preloaded before the first slide. The audience responded strongly, and it was reportedly still being talked about hours later. It was not a formal award, and I describe it as what it was: a well-received presentation.

## Options considered

**Build it as a gate.** The obvious design is a pass-or-fail check that blocks a release. A colleague team was building exactly that, across the bank. I did not compete with it. A gate is about breadth and compliance. What I was building was about depth and quality on generated output, with feedback that an agent can act on. I framed the two as tiers: theirs is the gate, mine measures quality and should feed their rules. That framing kept a possible turf argument from becoming one.

**A human review rubric.** Reviewing every generated screen by eye does not scale, and reviewers disagree. Machines are good at catching tokens and missing labels. People are good at taste. I made the checks catch the first so that reviewers could spend their time on the second.

**Only grade code that follows the system.** I rejected this. See the constraints.

## Key decisions

**Grade the output, not the model.** The checks score what an agent produced. That means the same checks work with any model, any prompt and any documentation, which is the whole point: a team can change one thing and see whether the score moved.

**Work on apps that were never built on the system.** This turned out to be the feature that made teams ask for it after the first demos, because those are the applications they most want to migrate.

**Report findings someone can act on.** A score alone is a verdict. A finding that says what is wrong, why it matters and what the system provides instead is something an agent, or a person, can act on. I learned that from the research on an internal Figma plugin and carried it over.

## Outcome

It is ready on web. Android and iOS are in progress. I presented it up the leadership chain as the measurement layer for AI-assisted UI work, and teams began asking for it after the first demos.

I am deliberately not publishing numbers here. The adoption and scoring figures have not been cleared for publication, so this page describes what it does and not how much.
